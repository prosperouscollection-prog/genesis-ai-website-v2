What a Missed Call Costs a Detroit HVAC Company

August 26, 2026

A Detroit HVAC company running one or two trucks typically fields 30 to 50 inbound calls a week once summer heat or winter cold hits. Miss even one in five of those calls, a rate that is easy to hit when every tech is on a roof or under a furnace instead of at a desk, and that is 6 to 10 calls a week going unanswered. At a typical service-call ticket of $250 to $400, that is $1,500 to $4,000 a week walking straight to a competitor. Stretch that across a full summer AC season and the number stops looking like a rounding error on the P&L.

Why the phone goes unanswered

Most HVAC crews run two to five people, and every one of them is usually on a job site somewhere between Warren and Dearborn when the phone rings. Nobody is sitting at a desk waiting to pick up. Calls that come in during a no-heat call at 7am or a compressor failure at 9pm hit voicemail instead, and voicemail does not book jobs. Even a full-time office manager only covers one shift, so early mornings and weekends still default to voicemail unless someone is specifically staffed for those hours.

The caller does not wait around

A homeowner with no AC on a 90-degree August day in metro Detroit is not leaving one voicemail and sitting by the phone. A quick search turns up eight or ten other HVAC companies within a 15-minute drive. If your line rings out, they call the next name on the list before your voicemail greeting finishes. For an emergency home repair, that is the default behavior, not the exception.

The math compounds over a season

Furnaces and compressors do not fail on a schedule. A large share of no-heat and no-cool emergencies come in nights and weekends, right when the field crew is off the clock and the office is closed, and those tend to be the most urgent and highest-ticket calls of the week. A single missed emergency call is bad enough on its own, but the real damage is cumulative. Detroit summers run roughly 90 to 100 cooling-degree days where AC calls spike, and winter brings a similar stretch of no-heat emergencies from December through February. Losing $1,500 to $4,000 a week is not a one-time event, it is a pattern that repeats every peak week of the year. Multiply that by 12 to 16 peak weeks a season and a shop can be leaving five to six figures of booked work on the table annually, work that went to whichever competitor happened to answer first.
